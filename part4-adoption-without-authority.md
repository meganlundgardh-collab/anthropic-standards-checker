# Part 4 — Adoption Without Authority

Adoption starts by making the standard cheaper to follow than to ignore.

**Show the concrete improvement, don't just dump findings.** Before asking a team to adopt anything, I'd run the checker against their own pages and bring them a concrete "before and after" — much like the Cowork Plugins rewrite in Part 2. Seeing a five-minute fix that replaces a drifted component list and resolves broken links lands as a low-cost win. A policy ask competes with their roadmap; a clear before/after doesn't.

**Reframe the ask around shared data.** Broken links and drifted definitions don't just hurt content quality; they drive up support tickets and cause search drop-off. By tying the checker's output to metrics that adjacent product and support leaders already track, the standard becomes a shared operational goal rather than just a content preference.

**Diagnose scope gaps before assuming resistance.** If a team looks like they are ignoring the standard, the first thing I check is whether the standard actually fits their surface. This exercise surfaced a real example of this: a literal reading of Rule 1 would have flagged Claude Tag's admin-scoped "connection" as drift, when it's a deliberate, correct distinction. A team failing to adopt is often signaling a structural mismatch, not noncompliance.

**Escalate via metrics, not authority.** If the standard genuinely fits and the team still won't move after seeing verified, low-cost findings, escalation goes through data tied to a metric their own leadership already tracks. The checker's output becomes the artifact I bring to that conversation, avoiding "the style guide says so" appeals entirely. Any real exceptions get documented (like Claude Tag), ensuring the standard stays honest about where it doesn't apply instead of silently degrading.
